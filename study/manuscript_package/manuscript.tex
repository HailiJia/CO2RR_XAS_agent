\begin{abstract}
Identifying adsorbates and local catalyst environments from X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) requires coordinated structure generation, electronic-structure calculations, spectral simulation, and statistical inference. These steps are often connected through task-specific scripts that make chemical labels and calculation provenance difficult to maintain. We present an agentic framework that connects natural-language planning to explicit tools for bimetallic interface construction, adsorbate placement, chemical validation, restartable high-performance computing, and reusable ISAAC records. The framework provides FEFF, FDMNES, and VASP input-generation routes and a configuration-aware machine-learning interface. A \ca{} demonstration evaluates adsorbate presence and depth sensitivity using simulated Cu K-edge spectra. An audited inventory contains 155 unique structures, including unfinished calculations, of which 87 independent configurations provide 348 compatible layer spectra. A nested independent-species tree workflow achieves pooled held-out micro and macro F1 scores of 0.779 and 0.638, respectively. With feature and threshold selection within each fixed depth, the top-layer micro F1 is 0.786, compared with 0.744 for the top-two-layer average and 0.659 for the four available layers. Rare species and coadsorbate omissions limit complete identity-set recovery. The results demonstrate a traceable route from controlled structural hypotheses to simulation-based spectral inference and identify the additional calculations needed for matched carbon-coupling and complementary-edge tests.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The interpretation of catalytic spectroscopy depends on connecting a measured signal to a distribution of atomic environments. For electrochemical \co{} reduction, this connection is complicated by the coexistence of adsorbed reactants, hydrogenated intermediates, oxygen-containing species, and spectator adsorbates. Different local environments can support similar molecular compositions, while the same intended adsorbate can adopt different binding motifs or dissociate during relaxation. Carbon--carbon bond formation provides a particularly demanding example: two neighboring CO molecules and a bonded OCCO intermediate contain the same elements but represent distinct chemical hypotheses. Calculations have shown that carbon coupling depends on adsorbate hydrogenation and surface structure, motivating explicit comparisons of competing local configurations rather than an assignment based on composition alone.\cite{montoya2013,montoya2015}

XAS offers element-selective sensitivity to electronic structure and neighboring atoms. Near-edge spectra can reflect changes in coordination, hybridization, and local symmetry, making them useful for testing atomistic models of catalytic interfaces. Their interpretation remains an inverse problem. The spectrum of a heterogeneous catalyst combines contributions from inequivalent absorbers, and surface-bound species can modify only a small fraction of the metal sites contributing to an experimental measurement. Consequently, a local spectral response can become weaker after averaging over subsurface or bulk-like environments. A model trained on an individual surface layer and one trained on an ensemble average answer different inference questions.

Computational XAS and machine learning provide complementary tools for this problem. FEFF and FDMNES calculate near-edge spectra using real-space formulations, while plane-wave approaches such as VASP provide routes based on core-excited supercells.\cite{rehr2010,joly2001,vasp1996,vaspxas} Lightshow standardizes input generation across spectroscopy codes.\cite{lightshow2023} Neural-network studies have established that simulation-trained models can recover structural descriptors from XAS, including in bimetallic catalysts.\cite{timoshenko2018,marcella2020} These advances leave a practical integration problem: the structure, the absorber population, the processing history, and the statistical sample must remain linked throughout a calculation campaign.

Large language models can translate research requests into tool calls and organize multistep tasks. Chemistry agents demonstrate the value of coupling such models to specialist software.\cite{bran2024} For computational spectroscopy, reliable use requires a clear boundary between planning and scientific validation. A plausible instruction does not establish that an adsorbate remained intact, that a spectrum represents a complete layer, or that validation folds contain independent structures. Those properties require explicit checks on the generated artifacts.

Here we develop an agentic framework connecting catalytic structure generation, relaxation, XAS simulation, ISAAC record construction, and machine-learning analysis. The framework records requested and observed chemical labels, preserves configuration linkage across derived spectra, and supports restartable execution on NERSC. We use \ca{} interfaces to demonstrate the workflow and evaluate adsorbate identity and layer sensitivity from Cu K-edge spectra. The broader design includes metal, C, and O edges and matched comparisons of coupled and uncoupled CO. The present quantitative results concern the completed Cu K-edge cohort; geometry prediction, coverage regression, combined-edge inference, and carbon-coupling discrimination remain subsequent validation targets.

\section{Methods and implementation}
\subsection{Agent architecture and scope of autonomy}
The framework separates request interpretation from deterministic execution (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). A planning layer converts natural-language requests into a schema-validated plan containing structural and spectroscopic settings. The implementation supports an OpenAI-compatible model endpoint, with the model specified through configuration rather than fixed in the workflow. Explicit application controls and deterministic commands provide additional entry points. Structure builders, input generators, scheduler interfaces, parsers, and machine-learning routines execute the resulting operations.

This separation makes the scope of autonomy inspectable. The planning layer can select supported operations and pass their arguments; numerical calculations are performed by the scientific backends. Chemical screening and output validation determine whether their products can be used downstream. Restart behavior is represented in persistent workflow state. The current implementation therefore supports automated task execution and recovery within defined routes. The dataset and prediction results reported here do not, by themselves, establish an unattended closed loop that chooses experiments or optimizes catalytic performance. A production comparison of language models, prompts, and intervention rates requires execution logs beyond the present spectral cohort.

\subsection{Bimetallic interfaces and adsorbate controls}
The structure-generation tools support pure-metal surfaces and bimetallic configurations. The generalized lateral builder accepts distinct metals with matching fcc(111) facets and ordered metal identities, denoted M1 and M2. Available controls include slab dimensions, layer counts, stripe widths, and adsorption regions. Boundary rows, neighboring rows, and terrace regions are defined relative to the periodic interface. Explicitly unsupported facets, unavailable rows, and incompatible metal-specific requests raise errors. Tests documented with the implementation cover Cu--Au, Cu--Ni, Cu--Pt, and Ni--Pt placements; they establish software behavior rather than thermodynamic stability or spectral transferability.

Adsorbate templates and scenario generators provide monodentate, bridging, and coupled configurations. Region selection is distinct from binding geometry: a species can occupy an interface region through an atop or multicoordinated motif. Distribution controls create approximately uniform, interface-biased, terrace-biased, or coadsorbed arrangements. Placement is deterministic under the specified controls. For coupled OCCO placements, the molecular C--C geometry is preserved rather than stretched to match a pair of metal sites. The dictionary generator retains COCO as a legacy alias for OCCO; chemical interpretation must therefore use molecular connectivity and atom mapping rather than treating every input name as a distinct species.

The planned \ca{} campaign spans CO, activated or molecular \co{}, hydrogenated C\textsubscript{1} intermediates, oxygen- and hydrogen-containing adsorbates, and C\textsubscript{2} intermediates. Its axes include interface versus terrace placement, coverage, orientation, and coadsorption, with pure Cu(111), pure Au(111), and lateral stripe parents. The broader structural plan includes Cu:Au stripe ratios of 1:1, 2:1, 1:2, 3:1, and 1:3, with explicit row counts retained because a ratio alone does not specify the supercell. These controls define the intended sampling space, not completed calculations for every combination. The completed cohort is a subset of that space and is enumerated separately in Table~\ref{tab:inventory}.

\subsection{Coverage definitions and chemical labels}
Coverage is stored with its numerator, denominator, and reference basis. For molecular coverage of species $s$,
\begin{equation}
\theta_s^{\mathrm{mol}}=N_s/N_{\mathrm{ref}},
\end{equation}
where $N_s$ is its molecular count and $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the declared surface reference population. Occupied binding sites are tracked separately because a multidentate molecule need not occupy one metal atom. For the OCCO-plus-spectator-CO scenario, the existing carbon-equivalent convention is retained,
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{C}}=(2N_{\mathrm{OCCO}}+N_{\mathrm{CO}})/N_{\mathrm{ref}}.
\end{equation}
This definition allows equal carbon loading for 2CO and OCCO without implying equal molecular coverage. Rounded coverages in directory names are provenance hints rather than authoritative measured labels.

Schema 2.0 structure metadata records molecule identifiers, atom correspondence, requested species and geometry, configuration and parent identifiers, current geometry fingerprints, coverage definitions, and chemical quality-control status. Mappings are updated into the element-grouped POSCAR atom order and carried through relaxation. Periodic contact checks examine atom pairs, self images, molecular formulas, intended bonds and angles, unintended intermolecular contacts, and adsorbates below the selected face. The general geometry checker treats in-plane periodicity and a nonperiodic vacuum direction explicitly. These are configurable screening rules and do not substitute for an electronic bond-order analysis or a stability calculation.

The standalone cohort audit additionally reconstructs connected C/O/H components from the calculation-input structure. Pair cutoffs are 1.85, 1.70, 1.30, 1.20, 0.90, and 1.65~\AA{} for C--C, C--O, C--H, O--H, H--H, and O--O, respectively. Formula and connectivity distinguish, for example, CHO from COH and COOH from OCHO; the presence of an O--H connection distinguishes the corresponding protonation motifs. Accepted assignments remain unchanged when the cutoffs are scaled by 0.9 and 1.1. Unknown or ambiguous components are excluded rather than silently assigned their directory label. This lightweight legacy-data audit is distinct from the more general periodic geometry checker; convergence of their boundary handling is a code-reconciliation task.

\subsection{Relaxation and spectroscopy backends}
VASP provides the relaxation route, after which XAS inputs are regenerated from the final CONTCAR.\cite{vasp1996} Representative committed \ca{} relaxation decks use a 520~eV plane-wave cutoff, electronic convergence of $10^{-5}$~eV, a force criterion of 0.05~eV~\AA$^{-1}$, fixed cell vectors, and a $\Gamma$-point $1\times1\times1$ mesh. The supplied adjacent-CO example fixes the bottom three metal layers and permits the top two to relax. These settings describe the committed input example; a definitive methods archive must reconcile settings and convergence for every completed run, including PAW datasets, exchange--correlation choices, and executable versions. They are not inferred from a shared folder name.

FEFF, FDMNES, and VASP input-generation routes provide complementary numerical treatments (Table~\ref{tab:backends}). The completed multilabel benchmark uses FDMNES in Green-function multiple-scattering mode. All 348 layer input decks request SCF, Green, and Perdew, with a 6.0~\AA{} cluster radius and an energy range from $-5$ to $50$~eV in 0.2~eV increments. They omit explicit SCFexc, Screening, nohole, and Gamma\_hole controls. FDMNES revision 28 July 2026 is identified in surviving output banners from every layer. The 332 available convolution logs report Gamma\_hole=$1.55$~eV and Gamma\_max=$10$~eV. The corresponding FDMNES guide describes the default K-edge treatment as ground-state self-consistency followed by a core hole with screening charge introduced at the XANES step. The observed inputs and versioned outputs therefore support that default treatment for this cohort; the record field itself remains not_reported. Sixteen layer spectra lack a separate convolution log. These details distinguish the Green-function cohort from a finite-difference cohort and remain separate from a cross-backend benchmark.\cite{fdmnesmanual}

Each backend retains absorber, edge, input-structure, and processing provenance. A common configuration identifier links outputs but does not establish equivalent site sampling, core-hole approximations, or energy alignment across codes. C and O K-edge calculations belong to the planned adsorbate-sensitive comparison; no combined-edge performance is reported from the current Cu-only benchmark.

\subsection{NERSC execution and restart}
The workflow package contains relaxation inputs, post-relaxation XAS regeneration, submission scripts, a static manifest, and a persistent state file. Access to remote commands and files uses the NERSC Superfacility API.\cite{nersc} Slurm dependencies connect successful relaxation to the XAS continuation. Consequently, the continuation does not depend on an open web application session. It requires a nonempty relaxed structure, regenerates the selected XAS stages, and records job identifiers and status.

Restart routines inspect existing stages and scheduler state, continue incomplete work, and avoid duplicating jobs already recorded as active or complete. Output checks and available recovery handlers provide additional validation. Scheduler completion, numerical validity, record creation, and ML eligibility remain separate states. A job can finish without yielding a usable spectrum, and a valid spectrum can be unsuitable for a particular paired-layer analysis. The present inventory therefore reports observable record and cohort counts rather than an inferred autonomous-execution success rate.

\subsection{ISAAC records and spectral population audit}
ISAAC records organize material composition, geometry, computational context, spectral series, processing information, and asset links. A configuration identifier connects layers, absorbers, and backend variants from the same physical configuration. Requested and observed identities remain distinguishable. For mixtures, species counts and identity sets are explicit: CO plus H is a multilabel state containing two components, whereas CHO is one bonded component. Metadata used to construct or audit these labels is not supplied as an input feature to the spectral classifier.

The completed cohort is read from \texttt{measurement.series}. Each usable record must contain finite, nonconstant, compatible Cu K-edge spectra for layers 2--5. Layer 5 is the physical top; this ordering is checked against absorber heights and adsorbate location. Record arrays are compared with the convolved output files, and input elements and coordinates are compared with the actual calculation structure. Absorber tables, selected sites, and final convolution populations are reconciled for each layer. Identical geometries and duplicate complete spectral stacks are removed before model evaluation.

This population audit is essential when a directory contains subset recalculations. Two CO cases had three-site main decks or intermediate logs but final convolution outputs representing 36 sites. They were retained only after the audit matched the final-convolution sites to raw spectra using energy-cutoff agreement, complete atom coverage, and compatible grids. The correction changed their eligibility annotations, not the measured arrays. One further CO configuration remains excluded because its layer-5 grid is incompatible with the paired-layer representation.

For compatible raw layer means $\mu_l(E)$ representing $n_l$ Cu sites, the depth representation is
\begin{equation}
\mu_D(E)=\frac{\sum_{l\in D}n_l\mu_l(E)}{\sum_{l\in D}n_l}.
\label{eq:average}
\end{equation}
Population weighting occurs before normalization. The analysis compares four individual layers and top-two, top-three, and four-layer averages. The last representation contains the four available Cu layers; it is not an all-slab or macroscopic bulk average.

\subsection{Spectral features and multilabel models}
The benchmark predicts presence or absence for each observed adsorbate species. Neither molecular counts, coverage, adsorption geometry, nor structural descriptors are predictor inputs. Spectra lie on the common recorded energy grid from 8974.0 to 9029.0~eV. Each representation is shifted by its minimum and normalized by its integrated area. Fixed per-spectrum transformations include normalized shape, cumulative integral, first derivative, a concatenation of smoothed shape, cumulative integral, and first and second derivatives, and 24 discrete-cosine coefficients. Savitzky--Golay derivatives use an 11-point window and a third-order polynomial. Additional tree-based candidates use multiscale derivative windows of 7, 11, and 21 points or concatenated top-layer and top-minus-subsurface channels.

Models comprise balanced logistic regression ($C=1$), an RBF support-vector machine ($C=3$), joint multi-output random forests and extremely randomized trees, and independent binary extremely randomized trees for each species.\cite{geurts2006} Joint forests use 96 trees; independent classifiers use 64 trees per species. Scaling for logistic regression and SVM is fitted only on the training partition. The SVM decision function is transformed by a sigmoid for thresholding; it is not a calibrated probability. Constant-label training cases receive constant scores. The executed benchmark does not use PCA, even though the general application interface offers that option.

\subsection{Grouped evaluation and automatic selection}
Five outer folds hold out whole configurations. The 85 configurations retained from the preceding analysis keep their fold assignments; two recovered configurations are allocated using labels and fold counts, without using spectra or prediction scores. Outer test sizes are 17, 17, 17, 18, and 18 configurations. All layer representations of a configuration share its fold. Three inner folds select features and decision thresholds using only outer-training configurations. Candidate inner assignments are chosen to reduce missing-positive training partitions and label imbalance, using training labels alone. The saved manifests provide the precise assignments.

The primary workflow selects among independent-species tree representations within each outer-training partition. Global threshold candidates are 0.15, 0.25, 0.35, 0.45, 0.50, 0.55, 0.65, and 0.75. Guarded species-specific adjustments are considered only with sufficient positive and negative training examples and shrink toward the selected global threshold. Selection objectives include micro and macro F1; the primary reported procedure uses the micro objective. The depth comparison fixes a depth representation and selects features and thresholds within it. Pooled held-out predictions give micro F1, macro F1 across all 11 species, and exact identity-set accuracy.

Conditional 95\% intervals use 1500 bootstrap resamples of held-out configurations and the saved fitted predictions. Paired depth comparisons use the same resampled configurations. These intervals do not include model-refitting variability or corrections for multiple comparisons. The cohort has also informed previous exploratory analysis; nested selection does not turn it into an external confirmation set. A separate ranking identifies the best observed fixed model/feature/depth entry after inspecting outer scores. Its performance is reported as exploratory.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Controlled dataset generation and usable cohort}
The inventory audit identifies 172 source locations and 155 unique physical structures, including unfinished calculations and one standalone CuO reference. Source structures with identical coordinates count once, with their aliases preserved. Copies of a structure in calculation or layer folders do not create independent samples. For inventory purposes an empty CONTCAR falls back to a readable POSCAR; this does not imply a completed relaxation. Figure~\ref{fig:dataset} shows both the full inventory and the ML-eligible subset.

There are 102 completed-record files in the audited source set. Fourteen duplicate records are removed, and one record is excluded for the incompatible paired-layer grid, leaving 87 independent configurations and 348 layer spectra. Thus the statistical sample size is 87 rather than 348. Seven regenerated records required corrections to component identities, counts, or site-species labels. Their measurement arrays were preserved. For example, a directory describing intended CHO can yield disconnected CO and H in the calculation structure. Treating the directory name as an intact-CHO target would train the model against an incorrect chemical assignment.

The 11 completed species are CHO, CO, \co{}, COCHO, COH, COOH, H, O, OCCOH, OCHO, and OH. CO has 30 positive configurations and H has 22; \co{}, COCHO, and OCCOH have only 3, 1, and 4, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}). Nine configurations contain mixed identity sets: five CO+H, three CHO+CO+H, and one CO+OH. The remaining 78 contain a single observed species. No completed clean controls enter the benchmark. OCCO-containing structures occur in the full inventory, but there are no usable intact-OCCO positives in the 87-configuration cohort. This distinction constrains the carbon-coupling claim directly.

\subsection{Reference benchmarks and complementary spectral channels}
Cu, Cu\textsubscript{2}O, and CuO provide a planned reference set spanning metallic, Cu(I), and Cu(II) environments. Their principal roles are to evaluate spectral processing and energy alignment and to provide reference shapes for subsequent mixture analysis. They should not be treated as a universal conversion between an arbitrary interface spectrum and a formal oxidation state. Adsorbates, coordination changes, and averaging can alter near-edge shape without corresponding to a simple mixture of bulk reference phases.

Figure~\ref{fig:spectroscopy}a retains the available CuO comparison among experiment, FDMNES Green-function and finite-difference modes, and FEFF. The supplied normalized curves show an overall absorption rise and differences in near-edge and post-edge shape. Complete trace-level experimental provenance, alignment, normalization, and broadening recipes are needed to calculate reproducible benchmark metrics. We therefore do not infer RMSE, correlation, or a backend ranking from the image. The Cu and Cu\textsubscript{2}O reference comparisons remain to be consolidated under the same protocol.

For the intended quantitative reference audit, the comparison should report the common energy interval, the permitted rigid shift, any fitted scale or offset, normalized RMSE, Pearson correlation, and edge- or peak-position differences. Parameters used to align a reference should be distinguished from prediction accuracy. A backend whose spectrum has been shifted and broadened specifically to fit a test trace should not be described as providing an unadjusted absolute-energy prediction.

The framework also exposes routes for C and O K edges. These edges are intended to complement the metal response by probing adsorbate-centered unoccupied states and local oxygen coordination. Their added information is a hypothesis to evaluate on matched configurations. No available cross-edge cohort supports a numerical gain from combining Cu, C, and O spectra at this stage.

\subsection{Layer-resolved response and descriptive identity contrasts}
Representative CO spectra show that layer-resolved Cu K-edge curves retain similar broad shapes while differing in the near-edge rise and higher-energy features (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectroscopy}b). These are actual recorded layer means, not simulated illustrative curves. Their similarity explains why absorber selection and quantitative population accounting matter: a small shape difference can carry useful information even when it is less conspicuous than the common metallic response.

The full-cohort top-two-layer presence-minus-absence contrasts provide a descriptive view of spectral variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectroscopy}c). The strongest separated local maxima of the root-mean-square contrast across species with at least five positive configurations occur at 8978.8, 8981.8, 8991.2, and 8998.2~eV on the recorded simulation scale. H-containing configurations show a negative early-region contrast, while OH shows a positive contrast around 8991~eV. Several carbon-containing species share broadly similar patterns.

These differences are not unique adsorbate fingerprints. The positive and negative groups are not matched in coverage, geometry, or coadsorbate composition, and all can contribute to the contrast. The heatmap is neither model attribution nor a causal separation of adsorbate effects. Its energy windows were not fed back into model selection. Its role is to motivate controlled calculations that isolate the source of the observed variation.

\subsection{Multilabel adsorbate inference}
The primary independent-species tree selector achieves micro F1 0.779, macro F1 0.638, and exact identity-set accuracy 0.690 from pooled outer-fold predictions. This procedure selects representation and thresholds within the outer-training data. It provides the main quantitative evidence for automated spectral inference in the present cohort (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}a,b).

Performance varies substantially by species. CO has F1 0.929 with precision 1.000 and recall 0.867 across 30 positive configurations. H has F1 0.757 across 22 positives. O and OCHO reach F1 0.833 and 0.800, respectively, with seven and eight positive configurations. CHO has F1 0.286 with only one of five positives detected. COCHO receives F1 zero: its single positive configuration cannot provide a positive training example when that configuration is held out. Its failure is consequently a sampling limitation as well as a prediction outcome.

The higher micro than macro F1 reflects stronger aggregate performance on common species and failures on less represented labels. Exact identity-set accuracy is stricter because a missing spectator species makes the entire predicted set incorrect. Reporting these three measures together distinguishes successful detection of a dominant adsorbate from complete chemical-state identification.

The best observed fixed entry is joint extra-trees applied to the first derivative of the top-layer spectrum, with micro F1 0.798 and macro F1 0.660. Its exact identity-set accuracy is 0.701. This entry was identified after comparing outer-fold scores and is exploratory. The difference from the primary selector is not evidence that a single workflow will remain optimal as new configurations are added. Figure~\ref{fig:ml}d displays the fixed top-layer model/feature comparison to expose this exploration rather than hide the selection step.

\subsection{Surface sensitivity and averaging over available layers}
At fixed depth, independent-species trees with inner-selected features and thresholds give top-layer micro/macro F1 of 0.786/0.643. The corresponding values are 0.707/0.563 for layer 4, 0.407/0.265 for layer 3, and 0.384/0.281 for layer 2. For population-weighted averages, the top-two, top-three, and four-layer values are 0.744/0.625, 0.670/0.528, and 0.659/0.508, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}c).

The top-layer micro-F1 difference relative to the four-layer average is 0.127, with a conditional paired 95\% interval of 0.069--0.191. Relative to the top-two-layer average, the difference is 0.042 and the interval is $-0.007$--0.091. Thus the present comparison supports a loss of inference performance when the deeper available layers are included, while the smaller top-versus-top-two difference remains uncertain under this conditional analysis.

This trend is consistent with surface-signal dilution within the sampled structures. It does not establish the depth response of an experimental detection mode or the magnitude of dilution in a macroscopic catalyst. The four-layer average excludes the unsampled bottom layer and has no attenuation, particle-size, orientation, or detection-efficiency weighting beyond the Cu populations in Eq.~\ref{eq:average}. More realistic ensemble modeling is needed before selecting an experimental geometry from this comparison.

\subsection{Coadsorbates and unmeasured inference tasks}
Mixture diagnostics reveal a weakness hidden by common-species performance. For the exploratory fixed winner, H is detected in all 14 single-species H configurations but in only one of eight mixtures containing H. OH is detected in three of four single-species configurations and zero of one mixture. CHO is detected in one of two single-species cases and zero of three mixtures. These small counts cannot determine universal limits, but they identify a specific need for matched coadsorption data. A CO-containing spectrum can be classified correctly for CO while still failing to recover the full identity set.

The metadata and general ML builder support additional potential targets, including geometry and realized coverage. The executed cohort analysis uses them only for auditing. No geometry accuracy, coverage error, count-regression score, combined-edge gain, noise tolerance, or calibrated uncertainty is reported here. The natural-language agent and web route now call a shared audited species-presence API that keeps the archived nested selector separate from a newer training-only global-threshold option. Saved-model replay checks implementation behavior on the local cohort export; this does not establish generalization beyond the repeatedly examined 87-configuration cohort.

\subsection{A matched hypothesis test for carbon coupling}
The proposed mechanistic demonstration asks whether XAS can distinguish two uncoupled CO molecules from an intact OCCO intermediate at equal carbon and oxygen loading, and whether the response differs between Cu-side interface and Cu terrace environments. Figure~\ref{fig:coupling} shows four actual input structures from the repository. Their initial minimum C--C separations are approximately 2.52 and 2.46~\AA{} for the two adjacent-CO arrangements and 1.45~\AA{} for each OCCO input. These values describe the constructed starting geometries, not relaxed bond lengths.

The comparison requires a shared parent, equal elemental loading, the same numerical settings, verified relaxed connectivity, and consistent absorber populations. Molecular coverage must remain distinct from carbon-equivalent coverage. The planned difference-in-differences contrast is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\rm int}(E)=\left[\mu_{\rm OCCO}(E)-\mu_{2\rm CO}(E)\right]_{\rm interface}
-\left[\mu_{\rm OCCO}(E)-\mu_{2\rm CO}(E)\right]_{\rm terrace}.
\end{equation}
This quantity tests whether coupling changes the calculated spectrum differently in the two environments. It is a proposed analysis, not a result from the present cohort.

Relaxation can change the hypothesis itself. If an intended OCCO structure separates into two CO components, its final record belongs to the uncoupled state. Such an outcome should be reported, including its initial label and final connectivity, rather than retained as an OCCO positive to balance a classifier. Because the eligible cohort has no intact-OCCO positives, it currently cannot establish 2CO/OCCO discrimination. Neither spectral separation nor a stable relaxed OCCO configuration alone would demonstrate a lower coupling barrier or improved C\textsubscript{2} selectivity. Those claims require energetics or kinetic evidence in an appropriate electrochemical model.

\subsection{Execution evidence and record reuse}
The repository provides implemented restartable workflow routes and example outputs for all three spectroscopy backends. The audited Cu K-edge records demonstrate reusable spectral content and configuration linkage. The available inventory does not provide a complete denominator of submitted jobs, retries, scheduler failures, or human interventions. Table~\ref{tab:reliability} therefore distinguishes record-level evidence from the production-execution benchmark that remains to be collected.

ISAAC validation/upload and retrieval clients provide the route to record deposition and reuse. Local JSON or ZIP ingestion permits analysis without portal credentials. The model interface can export fitted preprocessing, estimator information, dataset fingerprints, and split provenance. A repository record and an upload-capable client do not establish that the entire cohort has been deposited and retrieved independently. Local fixture tests exercise the round-trip comparison logic for stable identifiers, measurement hashes, labels, and saved-model predictions. No remote upload or independent portal retrieval is included in the present benchmark.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Traceability as a requirement for spectral inference}
The central contribution is the connection between a structural hypothesis and the exact spectral and statistical objects used to test it. Chemical labels, absorber populations, and configuration groups are integral to this connection. The label corrections demonstrate that an apparently straightforward training target can be wrong even when a calculation and its spectrum are valid. The population correction demonstrates the converse: an intermediate deck can suggest incomplete sampling while a valid final convolution contains the full layer. Tracking the artifact that actually produced the spectrum is therefore more reliable than adopting a single filename or status flag as proof of validity.

The current Cu K-edge results establish that adsorbate presence can be inferred to a useful, species-dependent extent within this simulated \ca{} cohort. The layer comparison shows that the representation of the absorber ensemble changes this inference. These observations provide concrete criteria for choosing subsequent calculations: expand independently sampled rare species, add clean controls, and create matched coadsorbate states at comparable geometry and coverage. More complex models cannot replace missing positive training examples or resolve mislabeled chemistry.

\subsection{From predictive performance to catalytic questions}
A useful catalytic inference model should distinguish the hypotheses relevant to the question being asked. For coadsorption, that may require simultaneous recovery of CO and H rather than classification of the dominant molecular group. For carbon coupling, it requires verified separation of uncoupled and bonded configurations under matched conditions. For catalyst design, it further requires evidence connecting those configurations to stability, accessibility, or reaction kinetics. The framework can organize these comparisons, but a prediction score by itself does not complete that causal chain.

The matched 2CO/OCCO design makes this distinction testable. Cu K-edge spectra can probe the response of nearby metal atoms, while the planned C and O edges provide additional adsorbate-centered channels. A negative result would also be informative: if the matched spectral contrast remains smaller than realistic noise and alignment uncertainty, the appropriate conclusion may be that the selected measurement representation cannot discriminate the two states under those conditions. That conclusion would guide an alternative edge, absorber subset, or complementary observable rather than an unsupported mechanistic assignment.

\subsection{Generality and experimental transfer}
Generalized fcc(111) interface generation expands the framework's structural scope, but the present spectral evidence is specific to the sampled \ca{} systems and processing conditions. Configuration-level folds prevent leakage of the same structure's layers across training and testing. They do not establish transfer to unseen parent interfaces, stripe ratios, facets, catalyst materials, or experimental spectra. Parent-family and condition holdouts are needed to test those forms of generalization.

Experimental application adds uncertainty from energy alignment, instrumental and lifetime broadening, orientation, detection mode, mixed phases, coverage distributions, and thermal dynamics. Electrochemical interfaces also require consideration of potential, solvent, electrolyte, and reconstruction. The current records do not establish an applied-potential or finite-temperature ensemble. Controlled noise, shift, and broadening perturbations should be evaluated on reserved configurations before comparison with experiment, with calibration and out-of-distribution rejection assessed separately from F1.

The versioned cohort manifest preserves final coordinates, spectra, and frozen folds. Surviving logs identify the FDMNES revision and convolution settings, while unavailable logs are reported as such.

\section{Conclusions}
We present an agentic framework connecting catalytic interface construction, chemical screening, relaxation, XAS simulation, reusable ISAAC records, and configuration-aware inference. A \ca{} Cu K-edge demonstration contains 87 audited independent configurations and 348 layer spectra. The primary nested multilabel workflow reaches micro/macro F1 of 0.779/0.638. The top layer has the highest point estimate among the evaluated fixed-depth representations, with a reduction to micro F1 0.659 after averaging the four available layers. Sparse positives and missed coadsorbates limit complete identity-set prediction. The framework makes these limitations actionable through explicit provenance and targeted sampling controls. Completion of clean references, matched coupled/uncoupled configurations, complementary edges, and execution/retrieval benchmarks will extend the demonstrated identity-and-depth workflow toward controlled \rr{} hypothesis testing.
